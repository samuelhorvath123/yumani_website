# Yumani Automation design system

## Scene and direction
A business or institution leader opens the site on a laptop in a bright office, looking for a capable team that can make everyday work easier. The page should feel like soft morning light: clear, optimistic and calm.

## Palette
A light icy-blue environment, anchored with cobalt blue. All neutrals are tinted toward blue. Tokens live in app/globals.css and use OKLCH: background 0.985 / 0.005 / 255; foreground 0.29 / 0.07 / 260; primary 0.55 / 0.206 / 264; muted text 0.485 / 0.035 / 260. A deep blue values section gives the longer page rhythm.

The colour arrives as a slow field rather than as decoration. One .bloom layer per atmosphere holds four wide, low-chroma blobs: cool blue at the lower left, periwinkle at the upper right, a pale cyan edge, and a light wash that keeps the copy on near-white. The field is visibly present but never bright, and it drifts on a 54-second cycle so the page reads as lit rather than painted. That drift is translate and a gentle rise in opacity only: a scale there would make Safari re-rasterise three section-sized layers on every frame for a 5% breath nobody can see. Reduced motion holds the field still. Services repeat the same hues at roughly four fifths of the hero, and the closing invitation carries a quieter pair; the footer stays plain so the page keeps its breathing room. The approach is the page's second deep-blue field, held still (two soft glows, no drift) because nothing behind it needs to move for the strand in front of it to be seen. The mesh uses interrupted lines with a detached dot at each crossing, in public/images/yumani-grid.svg. Cells scale from 104 to 144px. It is a whisper now, 0.08 on the lines and 0.13 on the dots, so it reads as paper texture under the light instead of as structure. Every background layer fades at section boundaries. The drift advances in quarter-pixel steps, a few times a second, rather than continuously: a continuous drift made the compositor redraw the whole section on every display refresh for motion of about a pixel a second, which was most of the GPU the page used in Safari.

## Typography
Manrope, served locally. Its open rounded forms, crisp structure and modest quirks bridge technical precision and approachability. Display type uses medium weight and close tracking, set in em so the ratio holds at every size in the clamp; desktop hero type grows fluidly to 78px, a section statement is 47px, and the one sentence the page is built around reaches 78px on two lines. Supporting copy sits at 15px on a 1.7 line height, which is larger and tighter than the 13 to 14px at 1.85 we began with, and closer to what the reference sites set. Headings that carry structure rather than meaning sit in --heading-quiet (7.1:1 against the page), so the two statements stay the loudest things on the page. A second voice, --font-figure (the system monospace), is reserved for the register figures in the footer: a number that should read as a number, never prose. It no longer sets the step numbers in How we work: a small monospace "01" in a system font was the one generic thing in that section, so the steps are numbered 1, 2 and 3 in Manrope itself at display size, where the number is a piece of typography and not a label. Avoid gradient type and decorative monospace.

## Layout
1280px maximum content width with generous outer margins. Left-aligned hero copy, original airy blue ribbon artwork on the right. Services open with the heading alone (an earlier paragraph and link under it said nothing the heading and the hero had not already said, so they were removed) and then the disclosure list, over a full-width blue atmosphere. Its independently generated panoramic ribbon continues the hero's gathered strand in one shallow wave from edge to edge. Desktop opacity is 34%, masked softer beneath the disclosures; mobile uses 22%. The decoration is absolutely positioned and clipped independently, so it adds no section height or horizontal scroll and does not clip focus rings. The values statement changes the atmosphere with a blue field. Process steps and the final invitation have distinct spacing. The page has a rhythm signature rather than a uniform one: the first long chapter (what we do) breathes at 200px top and bottom, the scale Raycast and Clerk keep, the chapters after it at 150px, because from there the page alternates light and deep blue and the change of colour does part of the separating, the values field holds 132px against its dark edges, and the closing invitation stays intimate at 108px. The who-this-is-for chapter is a band of four people rather than a set of cards, set apart from the about section above by the change of colour rather than by a rule, and it names the people who carry the work (the one who keeps the spreadsheet, the one in the middle, the one who signs it off, the one who is new) because we have no clients we are allowed to name.

The page then runs dark (about), light (who this is for), dark (how we work), light (straight talk and questions) and dark (the closing screen), instead of two long chapters of light on light. How we work is the second deep-blue field. Its three steps hang on one thin strand that carries on past the last of them, because the work does not end at launch, and each step names what it leaves you holding: three plain lines that restate promises the page already makes, never a result or a figure, set in the label and bullets the service rows use for "What that can look like". A line beneath the steps says what runs through all of them (the same small team, and one person from the client side for about an hour a week). The strand runs down the left at every width, with the steps beside it: on a wide screen each step is a row (its number and title, what we do, and what you get set off by a rule), on a narrow one they stack. The horizontal strand with three columns that this section first had was dropped for the vertical one, which is the layout that read best. The services ribbon is carried onto the blue above the first step and gathers toward the start of the strand; it steps aside where the steps stack. The strand is lit as far as the reading line, 62% of the way down the window, and a step is reached when its node crosses that same line, so the lit strand and the lit node are one fact told twice: where the browser supports scroll-driven animation the strand's fill is drawn by the scroll up to that line (the timeline is given no inset, because by default it takes the page's scroll-padding-top and would end the lit strand 80px below the line), on the compositor and without script. A short script (lib/process-steps.ts for the logic and its tests, lib/process-watch.ts for the DOM, app/process-steps.tsx to mount it) adds what a scroll timeline cannot, because it takes its own time. It says when each step is reached and the page answers: a ring leaves the node, the number warms from a ghost of the field to the strand's own light, the dots beside "What you get" light one after another, and a spark of light runs down the strand to the next node, the way light runs through the hero's ribbon (the last one runs on into the tail, since the work does not end at launch). Steps not yet reached are ghosts of themselves (dim node, faint number, unlit dots) but their words stay at full strength: nothing here dims text. A page that is opened part-way down settles without playing anything, and several steps reached in one jump arrive in order, 420ms apart. One switch per step, --lit, drives every part that changes, so they cannot disagree, and the pointer lights a step it rests on. Everywhere else the section still tells one story: without JavaScript it is complete, every step lit and the strand drawn in full; under reduced motion, and in a browser without scroll timelines, the strand is lit as far as the last step reached (the script measures it), at once under reduced motion, so it never says done while the steps beside it say not yet; reduced motion keeps the lit and ghost states, which are colour, and drops the ring and the spark, which are movement. The band is overflow: clip rather than hidden, because hidden would make it the scroller the timeline follows, and it never scrolls. Small type on the band takes a lighter accent than the headline (the headline's blue is 3.9:1 on this field, fine at 47px and not at 12px), and every text colour was measured against the brightest pixel behind it, glows and ribbon included, at 1440, 1100, 900 and 390px, with 4.5:1 as the floor.

Section edges are straight, on purpose. A shallow wave cut into the deep blue sections (the about section on both edges, the closing screen on its top) was built and then removed: it read as decoration, and this is a precise product, so a boundary should be a line. Do not bring it back. The looser, less boxed feel comes from stepping instead, and the step is regular rather than random: the first four rows of the service list and the four people in the who-this-is-for band take their position from one set of numbers (--stagger-1 to --stagger-4: 0, 0.62, 1, 0.5 of a full step). Each service row starts further in, by up to 96px, and each person sits lower in turn, by up to 48px, while the right-hand edge stays put so the toggles keep a line and the page keeps a spine. The step gives way on phones, where the band is one column and the rows lose most of their drift. Corners stay at two sizes.

Straight talk is the counterweight, in a chapter of its own: a good fit set against its opposite, four rows that read across as well as down (the columns share their rows through a subgrid), the left in cobalt and the right in muted blue-grey, with a tick and a cross rather than green and red. The four "not the right fit" statements are the deadline of a week, a decision made on price alone, nobody on the client side able to spare an hour a week, and AI because everyone else has it. Saying who the work is not for is rarer than saying who it is for, and it reads as confidence rather than as a pitch.

Questions worth asking before we start close the page's body, in the services list's own disclosure (the hairlines, the wash on the open row, the plus that turns into a minus) with six short answers, the first open. Every answer restates something the page already promises, so there is no price, timeline or result in them that the rest of the page does not stand behind. Questions the team has not yet decided, such as who owns the software or where it is hosted, are left out until there is an answer to give.

Mobile stacks sections and keeps the main navigation visible, as one row of three equal tabs under the wordmark (the section you are reading fills its own tab) rather than three words spread to the corners. The bar always fits: type eases down with the screen, and below 340px the language switch gives way, since there is no second language to switch to yet.

## Components and interactions
The logo is the lowercase yumani wordmark alone; there is no separate Y symbol. Hovering or focusing it lifts automation out of a clipped slot beneath the wordmark: it rises 108% of its own height over roughly 640ms on an ease-out curve, clears a 2.5px blur as it arrives, and settles from periwinkle into primary blue over about 800ms, echoing the hero rotation. The slot is absolutely positioned against the wordmark, so the reveal never shifts the header, the floating bar or the closing screen, and the at-rest mark stays optically centred. On wide screens the bar is pinned from the first pixel of scroll, so it begins gathering itself almost immediately instead of waiting for the hero to pass: 58px tall, the height n8n's own bar uses, reached the same way by stepping the wordmark, the links and the button down together rather than by trimming padding (about 97px on mobile, where the navigation keeps its own row and the change still waits until the hero is behind you). Its surface is not one flat white either: pale cyan at the left end, near-white through the middle and a breath of periwinkle at the right, which is the hero's own light carried onto the bar so it belongs to the atmosphere instead of sitting on top of it. The surface is 95% rather than fully opaque as well, a veil just heavy enough to notice when you look for it and light enough that the words passing beneath stay unreadable: measured across scroll positions the bar's own luma shifts by up to 8 of 255 depending on what is under it, while the detail inside it stays flat. The button keeps a white surface there, so it still reads as the one thing to press, and the corners stay inside the brand's two-token system: --radius-surface at 16px for the bar and --radius-control at 7px for every chip and button inside it. That is n8n's own ratio, a bar twice as round as the controls sitting in it, and it keeps the bar in flow with the buttons instead of inventing a third language. It also detaches from the content column: on wide screens it narrows to at most 1100px, a little inside the page's own margins, so it reads as an object rather than a band. Because the bar is already on screen when it converges, that narrowing is visible: each edge travels 90px inward over 780ms, the same order as deepseek.com's own 160px, and the contents inside travel with them. That is the difference between a bar gathering itself and a second bar appearing, and it is why the fade that used to hide the sideways movement is now kept only for small screens. Below 1051px the bar keeps the full content width, because the navigation has its own row there. One deliberate departure from n8n: their bar is a 20% veil of their dark brand colour, which works over a dark page. On a light page the same trick would ghost whatever scrolls beneath it, so this bar is opaque and takes its colour from the brand instead, with a hairline edge and a whisper of shadow doing the separating that their dark veil does for them. The pinning itself is applied from JavaScript rather than in the stylesheet, so the header stays a plain in-flow element for anyone without it, and the whole surface lives on a pseudo-element behind the bar so that it can arrive gradually without the bar's own geometry having to animate opacity. It does not swap between two sizes, it converges into them: the width travels from the content width to 1100px, the height from 112 to 58px, the wordmark from 31 to 25px and the button from 44 to 36px, all on one duration and one curve, with a beat of delay so the pull-in happens after the bar has faded in and can actually be seen. That is the whole reason the bar used to look like it simply appeared: the convergence was hiding behind the fade. On small screens the arrival is a short fade only, with no slide, because the convergence is the motion; on wide screens nothing arrives at all, the bar changes shape where it stands, and the fade lives on its surface instead, which comes up over 300ms as the edges start moving. The trick borrows from deepseek.com, whose bar narrows from 1140 to 980px and gains its frosted surface at a scroll threshold, though theirs keeps a constant height and uses a backdrop blur we deliberately refuse. The way back follows the manners of the way in: on wide screens the bar widens into the header again as the reader returns past the first 40 pixels of the page, and on small screens it fades out at its compact size and retakes its place in the page. It never scrolls the page itself: coming back up simply puts the bar back in its place and leaves the reader where they are. Keyboard focus reveals it the same way; reduced-motion swaps the rise for a short fade. Modest corners, and only two of them: --radius-control at 7px on everything actionable, from the navigation button, the navigation chips and both primary actions to the skip link and the service tags, and --radius-surface at 16px on the floating navigation bar alone. The service rows stay square; they are no longer the full width of their column, because each starts further in (above) and all of them end on the same right edge. The three navigation links are quiet chips rather than underlined words: the tint is the brand's own cobalt let down to a wash, so one rule reads correctly both on the pale hero and on the bar's tinted surface. The pointer gets that wash and the label settles into cobalt over 380ms; the section you are reading keeps the same wash at half strength, in place, and never changes its colour, so the bar answers "where am I" without raising its voice. In the hero, past the last navigation section and in the closing screen, no chip shows at all. The mark is a state, not a position: it fades where it belongs instead of travelling, which matters because the bar and the page are deliberately not in the same order, and a mark that moved would look broken while telling the truth. A reading line 40% down the viewport, never higher than the sticky clearance, decides which section is current, and it works from document order rather than a hard-coded list, so it follows the page even if sections move. It re-measures once per scroll frame, on resize and whenever the page changes height, which keeps it honest when a service disclosure opens or a font lands late. The logic lives in lib/section-spy.ts with its own tests, and app/main-nav.tsx only wires it to the DOM. On small screens the three links are one row of equal tabs under the wordmark rather than three words spread to the corners, and below 340px the language switch gives way so the bar always fits.

The team is a room that opens as you scroll into it, holding a shallow, concave stage. The room is a deeper, evening blue than the section around it, and it arrives as a rounded panel (the page's 16px surface corner) inset from the margins, with its heading, controls and stage drawn in to 84% of their size. As the carousel scrolls into view the panel widens to a full-bleed band, its corners flatten to nothing, and everything inside grows to full size, all tied to scroll position, so scrolling back closes it again. The opening runs from the moment the carousel first enters the screen until it is about two fifths of the way through, so it is finished before the portraits are read. Inside, the strip runs the full width under a header row (the heading and the arrows, with a one-pixel progress hairline in place of the words "Drag to explore"), snaps each person to the middle, and fades into the blue at both edges. The card in the middle faces you; its neighbours turn toward you by up to 30 degrees with a 1000px perspective and recede 40px, and the whole row rises to the middle in one shallow wave, the one the services ribbon draws. Depth is atmosphere rather than objects: a portrait that is not yet yours sits deeper in the room's blue, up to 70% of the way, and clears as it arrives. An earlier version fanned two sheets of tracing paper behind each card, slid the picture inside its frame and swept a band of light across it; it looked like a trick rather than the brand, so it is gone. Captions are deliberately kept out of the tilt, so the type is never rasterised at an angle, and they stay at full opacity, which on the room's blue puts the role line at 5.6:1 or better. The portraits take --radius-surface (16px), which brings them inside the two-corner system; before, they were the one square surface outside the service rows. None of it is JavaScript per frame. Every motion is a CSS scroll-driven animation (animation-timeline: view() on each card, a named view timeline on the carousel for the opening, scroll() on the strip for the thumb), transform and opacity plus the room's clip-path, with no backdrop blur. The opening needs the About section to clip with overflow: clip rather than hidden: hidden makes the section a scroll container, and the timeline would then be measured against it, not against the page, and sit finished from the start. Everything sits behind @supports and prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference, so without either the room is simply open and the strip flat and level with the same corners and shadow, and the progress hairline hides under reduced motion because the global rule would otherwise leave its thumb stuck at the start. The drag-to-scroll with coasting in lib/drag-scroll.ts is unchanged. Keyboard focus rings the card in the middle (3px, the page's focus colour), the one the arrow keys move between, instead of outlining the whole padded strip; where animation is off the strip keeps the standard outline. Until the portraits arrive the four slots hold a crop of the About artwork so the stage can be judged as it will read.

The page ends in one screen of evening light, in the spirit of the closing screen on wearecollins.com, where the last thing you meet is a single way in. It replaces the old closing invitation and the old footer together: the deep blue of the about section, a little deeper toward the bottom, with a periwinkle glow at the upper right and a cool one at the lower left. The invitation sits in the middle of it at headline size, "Let's make room for what's next.", with one button beneath it and nothing else: no section links, since the reader has just been through them, and no second route to the same place. The button is the hero's button exactly (the logo's cobalt hairline and label, the same wash on hover); only its surface is filled in with the same pale tint, because the hero's is mostly see-through and over this blue the label would be unreadable. The navigation bar steps aside here: it fades and is taken out of reach the moment this screen reaches the bar's lower edge, and returns as the reader backs out, so nothing floats over the invitation.

The company's details run along the bottom edge as the footer, kept to what is needed: two small lines carrying the trading name, the registered office, the company ID and the commercial register entry that Slovak law requires on a website, and one Legal button. Legal is the Let's talk button, defined once with the others, and opens upward into a floating surface built like the cookie banner (the pale glowing surface, the hairline, the 16px corner) whose rows are divided by the page's hairline with a small arrow that nudges on hover: Privacy Policy, Cookie Policy, Terms and Conditions and Cookie settings, with the promise of no advertising and no tracking without the reader's say-so beneath them. It closes on Escape, on a click elsewhere or on choosing a row, and it is a plain details element, so it works without a script. The footer is never hidden behind a reveal, so the identity is there the moment the page is. Every other page ends in the same blue foot on its own. The local time in Bratislava, the direct email link and the back-to-top link that used to sit here were removed to keep it clean; the address remains on the contact page and in the policies.

Lists answer the pointer with a spotlight, borrowed from the program list on wearecollins.com: the row under the pointer stays at full strength while the others settle back to 36% (the open service keeps 62%, because it is holding text), its title slides 10px to the right and the tagline 5px, so the two read as layers, and a 1px line of cobalt draws across the row's divider, entering from the left and leaving to the right so a pointer moving down the list passes the line on. It lives on the service accordion and the four people in the who-this-is-for band. It has not been extended to the good-fit and not-a-fit columns, whose rows are read across as pairs, or to the questions below them. It is the only hover that moves a whole row of content (everything else that moves on hover is one small mark, such as the wordmark's descriptor or an arrow), and it is a reply to the pointer rather than an arrival, so it is quick: 340ms for the fade and 460ms for the slide and the line, on the same exponential curve as the arrival but with nowhere near its duration. The tokens are --spot-ease, --spot-time, --spot-fade, --spot-slide, --spot-floor and --spot-floor-open in :root. Two numbers drive every part: --spot on the list ("something is being pointed at") and --on on a row ("this one"), and each part works out its own opacity, slide and line from them, so the effect is written once per list rather than once per state. They are only ever set under (hover: hover) and (pointer: fine), so a touch screen, where :hover sticks after a tap, never dims anything; keyboard focus on a service sets the row's own switch only, so the focused row slides and draws its line (beside its focus ring) but the others are not dimmed: a dim that lasts as long as focus rests there would hold text below readable contrast for a keyboard user, which a pointer passing over a row never does. What dims is each row's content, never the row itself, because a row's own opacity belongs to the arrival animation (and the panel's to opening and closing). The audience columns are 40px apart, so each one's hit area reaches halfway across the gap and the spotlight hands over from one to the next instead of letting go between them. The dim is a deliberate tradeoff: at 36% a dimmed title is 2.1:1 and a dimmed line of muted text 1.7:1 against the page, below WCAG's 3:1 and 4.5:1, and small muted text would need a floor of 0.87 to pass, which leaves no spotlight. It is accepted because it is momentary and pointer-only, because the row being pointed at is always full strength, and because keyboard focus never gets it and more-contrast gets a much lighter one. Raise --spot-floor if it ever has to hold more. Reduced motion keeps the dimming and the line, which are not movement, and drops the slide; more contrast keeps 80% of a dimmed row (90% for the open service) rather than 36% (62%), which is the least that holds body text at 4.5:1.

A service opens in 460ms (its fade in 380ms), the same curve as before at about 70% of the time: 650ms read as waiting. The questions further down the page are built the same way but keep their own timings in faq.css, so they still open at the earlier 650ms; they have not been retuned to match. Its icon draws itself as it opens, and that is paced separately and more slowly, because a line drawing is read, not just seen. The curve (--trace-ease) is prompt, then steady, then a short clean settle: half drawn at 36% of the time, nine tenths at 70%, peak speed 1.6 times the average. It was tuned twice. The first version used the page's expo-out curve, which is half drawn at 13% and nine tenths at 37%, so a 1050ms animation was over in about 400ms: quick and soft. The second was near-linear and took up to 1150ms, which was too slow. Each icon takes the time its stroke deserves, roughly 380ms plus 4.5ms for every unit of line, so a long stroke is not rushed, and it is set per icon from data-kind in services.css. The draw works as one dash along each stroke, and only when each stroke is its own path: strokes that share a path are each a fraction of it and all finish within the first few percent of the animation, which looks like a flash followed by nothing (the first Custom software icon did exactly that). The dash is one long with a gap of two, so a stroke that has not started yet leaves no dot at its end.

The four icons say what each service is, in one language: a 1.6 stroke with round caps, open contours, a pale offset fill that turns a deeper blue when the service is open, and filled or ringed points like the dots of the background mesh. Web applications is a browser window; Custom software is a card with its dimensions drawn on it, because software made to measure is what the service is and "when ready-made tools don't fit" is its problem; System integration is a strand joining two points, the ribbon's own gesture and the only icon about connection; Practical AI is a page with one line picked out and a small spark, because the service reads documents and pulls out the details. The first set had two icons that said the same thing (a record with data flowing in and out, and a strand between two points), one that read as code, and one, two panels split by a wavy gap, that could have been a book or a puzzle and said nothing about AI. Each draws itself in its own order: the window's bar crosses as the frame closes round it (600ms); each dimension line of the card is measured as a draughtsman would, an arrowhead, the line across, the far arrowhead, on both axes at once (470ms); the strand is one stroke (520ms); the page is drawn round, its folded corner when the line reaches it, and the spark arrives as the line settles (840ms).

Shadcn/Base UI accordion for service detail, native anchors for navigation and email. Clear focus rings, skip link, semantic section labels. The hero reads “Become the [business] of the 21st century.” The rotating word unfolds in a gentle letter wave, shifting from soft periwinkle into primary blue over roughly 1.5 seconds, followed by about three seconds for reading. Each word change sends light through the four separate strands of the hero artwork and into its unified ribbon. The first rotation begins within the first second of viewing the hero, and the cadence never changes: the word is a statement, not a control, so it has no hover state, no speed-up, and no glow or underline, and the ribbon answers only to the word change itself. The rotator reserves the width of the longest word so shorter ones never shift the line. Live reduced-motion support keeps the effect optional; offscreen or hidden-page rotation is suspended. The hero atmosphere fades into the page background at its lower edge.

Arrival is one gesture repeated at two scales, deliberately unhurried: depth carries the movement, so the eye follows it up into the page. The hero cascades in on load, headline then description then actions, each starting 44px lower and rising over 2100 to 2580ms on an ease-out curve; the artwork settles from 48px and 94% scale over 3750ms so it reads as coming forward rather than sideways. The navigation bar belongs to that first moment and fades in on the same 2100ms curve, but holds perfectly still: no rise, no scale, and the fade sits on its contents so the sticky bar's own arrival keeps the header's transform to itself. Below the hero, every block of content rises into place once, slowly, as it reaches the viewport: 56px over 2640ms on the same curve, with items inside a group staggered 270ms apart so a list settles rather than snapping. Because the curve is exponential, most of the distance is covered early and the last second is a slow settle; the page never feels stuck. The weights live as --rise, --rise-depth, --rise-stagger, --rise-hero and --rise-art in :root, so the whole page's arrival can be retuned from one place. The hidden state is a rule the bootstrap in app/layout.tsx injects before the body paints, so the page stays complete without JavaScript, in print and under reduced motion, and React hydration stays clean. Keyboard focus resolves a section in 160ms, so focus never rests on something invisible.

## Legal pages and cookie consent
Three plain-English pages live at /privacy, /cookies and /terms, built on one shell (app/legal-page.tsx, app/legal.css): a quiet bar with the wordmark, a heading, a contents list that stays beside the text on wide screens and sits above it on narrow ones, and numbered sections held to about 70 characters. Facts that read as a label and a value (the cookie record, the company, each way personal data is handled) share one column pattern, so a reader can compare them at a glance. The identity they quote comes from lib/company.ts, and the date at the top from the same file, so a change is made once. The text describes only what the site really does: no analytics, no third-party requests, one first-party cookie. If that changes, the pages change first.

The cookie banner is a small floating card in the lower left (full width on phones) in the same family as the floating navigation bar, with the same two corners. It appears a moment after the page has settled, only in the browser, and only when there is no valid choice on record. Reject all and Accept all are one style, one size and one weight, side by side, and Customise opens the optional categories in place. Nothing optional is on by default and nothing is stored before the visitor answers. Reopening it from the footer or from the Cookie Policy restores the earlier choice, moves focus into the card and returns it on close; Escape dismisses it. The one cookie, yumani_consent, holds the choice, the date and a version, for 180 days. The logic is in lib/consent.ts with its tests; app/cookie-consent.tsx only wires it to the DOM. Analytics is the one optional category, defined but unused: a tool added later reads the choice from the store and stays off until it is given, and CONSENT_VERSION is bumped whenever a category is added so everyone is asked again.

## Contact survey
Every contact button leads to /contact instead of opening an email app, which failed for anyone without one set up. The page is a calm heading beside one card that asks a question at a time: what you would like help with, what takes too long today, how soon you would like to start, and who to reply to. The first question takes the least effort and the last the most, so the visitor is well on the way when it counts; a progress bar of four segments (periwinkle when done, cobalt now) and a "Step n of 4" line say where they are. Choices are whole tiles with the Let's talk button's hairline and 7px corner, the same periwinkle wash on hover and a cobalt state with a tick when chosen; text fields share that hairline, a 16px size that stops phones zooming in, and the page's focus ring. The card is the 16px surface the navigation bar and cookie banner use. Moving on moves focus to the new question, errors name the problem in plain words and mark the whole group, and every answer stays in place when going back. A hidden field and a minimum fill time quietly turn away scripts, which are answered as if they had worked. If sending fails, the answers stay and the address is offered. The survey stores nothing in the browser, so it adds nothing to the cookie policy.

## Discoverability and housekeeping
The page carries an Organization record in JSON-LD using only facts already public in the commercial register: trading name, address, IČO as taxID, email, URL, founding date and language. robots.txt and sitemap.xml sit in public/, the social card at public/images/og.jpg is a real 1200 by 630 render of the current hero rather than the older artwork, and public/apple-touch-icon.png is the wordmark on the pale brand field for iOS home screens. The page colour is declared as the mobile theme colour. Working files that nothing loads (the two master artworks and the retired ribbon set) live in design/source/ so they stay in the repository without riding along in every deployment. A branded not-found page keeps a wrong URL inside the brand. Headings break evenly with text-wrap: balance and paragraphs avoid stranded last words with pretty; figures are tabular where numbers should line up; and visitors who ask their system for more contrast get firmer headings, text and rules. Every one of those degrades silently where a browser does not support it.

## Assets
design/source/yumani-flow-master.png is the custom transparent artwork: four fine strands resolve into one continuous blue ribbon. AVIF/WebP sizes supply responsive images and an alpha-derived luminance mask bounds the coordinated light effect. Generation prompt and motion notes live in design/hero-flow.md. The travelling light is built so the compositor can run it alone. Each highlight is a chain of solid discs whose union is the round-capped dash the design calls for; every disc follows the ribbon on precomputed transform keyframes (lib/flow-light.ts), each strand's chain is blurred by a CSS filter on its own layer, and a CSS alpha mask on the wrapper keeps the light on the ribbon. Nothing repaints while it runs, so it keeps full frame rate even when Safari halves the page's own rendering in Low Power Mode. The keyframes are thinned to those a straight segment needs to stay within about a device pixel of the ribbon, because WebKit re-evaluates every keyframe of every transform animation (each evaluation walking the list again) whenever it recomputes compositing; the animations are released as soon as a pulse ends for the same reason. Measured against the original SVG version (SVG blur filters under an SVG mask, re-rasterised on every frame): in WebKit the GPU process went from about 700 ms of CPU per second to about 15, pulses from roughly 15 frames a second to full rate, and it is visually identical to within a few levels. The mask is an AVIF that the hero only requests once the light can play, so it never competes with the artwork and fonts on first load, and reduced-motion visitors never download it. The word's letters arrive periwinkle and settle into cobalt through a filter (hue-rotate, saturate, brightness) solved to land on both tints, rather than an animated colour, for the same reason: filters animate on the compositor, colour only by repainting. The previous ribbon artwork is retained in design/source/ rather than shipped, so nothing unreferenced rides along in the deployment. Fonts are locally served Manrope weights with their OFL license. Until they arrive, text is set in Arial resized per weight to Manrope's proportions (the Manrope Fallback faces in globals.css), so the swap does not rewrap the headline or shift the page. The one exception is weight 800, which only the wordmark uses: its six letters are cut out of the original file (design/source/manrope-800.woff2, with the pyftsubset command in globals.css) and embedded in the stylesheet itself, about 1.5 KB, so the logo's font is there at the first paint on every page and navigation. A font file made the logo show in Arial until it arrived with font-display: swap, and vanish until it arrived with block, and both were visible; with no request there is nothing to wait for. The outlines and spacing are identical to the full weight. The favicon uses the wordmark, with no separate symbol.

design/source/yumani-services-ribbon-master.png is a separate transparent 3:1 artwork: the unified ribbon carries on horizontally across the services section. Responsive AVIF/WebP assets are 720, 1440 and 2160 pixels wide. Its generation prompt and placement notes live in design/services-ribbon.md.

Actions speak one language across the page: a solid cobalt button, or a quiet inline link. There is one button language, and it is the Let's talk button's: a 1px hairline, the 7px corner, a pale surface, a bold label, and a wash that arrives on hover in periwinkle and settles into cobalt while the label and arrow turn cobalt with it. Nothing lifts or moves on hover. It comes in two voices that share that anatomy and are defined together in globals.css. The standard voice (Let's talk, the cookie banner's Reject all and Accept all) has a white surface and a neutral blue hairline; the cookie pair must stay identical so neither answer is favoured. The main voice (the hero, the closing invitation, the contact survey's Next and Send it) speaks the same language in the logo's cobalt: the label and arrow are the wordmark's own colour (var(--primary)), the hairline is that cobalt at 45%, the surface is mostly see-through (a 3% cobalt tint over white at 60% opacity), so the hero shows through and the button does not glare; 60% is the least that keeps the label at 4.5:1 over the darkest part of the hero's blue on a phone, as measured from the rendered page. On hover the label takes the darker shade the wordmark takes when hovered, oklch(0.49 0.212 264), over the same 10% wash the standard voice gets. It reads as the one thing to press without becoming a solid block, and without a colour that exists nowhere else on the page. An earlier version used a deeper blue for the label and looked too dark beside the logo. Nothing lifts or moves on hover; the surface settles deeper and the arrow does the pointing. In the hero the pair shares a 54px height (50px on mobile) and only the first is a surface, so the fold makes one shape instead of two competing boxes, and the second path reads as a path rather than a second decision. Narrow layouts stack the button full width above the link without reducing label size, and reduced motion removes the arrow displacement. Existing labels and destinations are retained.
